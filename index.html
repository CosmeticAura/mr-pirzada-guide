Mr. Pirzada: techniques and conflicts

Literary techniques and internal and external conflicts in "When Mr. Pirzada Came to Dine" by Jhumpa Lahiri.

How to answerTechniquesConflictsSelf-check
How to answer any question: name the technique or conflict, point to the exact moment (candy, watch, pumpkin, Halloween, Mrs. Kenyon, the card), then say what it shows about the story's big idea: empathy, belonging, distance, growing up. End by linking to the ending: his family is safe, Lilia throws the candy away and misses him.

Internal and external conflicts

External

A struggle with something outside a person.
  • The 1971 war (East vs West Pakistan): his wife and 7 daughters are in Dacca.
  • Partition (1947) makes him 'not Indian'.
  • Lilia vs school: Mrs. Kenyon makes her put back the Pakistan book.
  • The outside world vs Lilia's safe life: smashed pumpkin, 'Indian witch'.

Internal

A struggle inside a person's mind.
  • Mr. Pirzada: fear for his family vs staying calm and polite at dinner (the watch).
  • Lilia: confusion about who is Indian and where she belongs.
  • Lilia: guilt and helplessness: she's safe, his daughters aren't; the candy and prayer are all she can do.
  • Lilia at the end: happy his family is safe, sad he's gone.

The external conflict ends with the war (Dec 16, Bangladesh, the card says they're safe). Lilia's internal conflict ends with her learning what it means to miss someone so many miles and hours away.

Literary techniques

Symbolism

An object that stands for a bigger idea.
  • Candy: Lilia's bond with Mr. Pirzada and her prayers. Thrown away when his family is safe, so the bond ends with it.
  • Watch on Dacca time: he lives in two places at once; his mind is with his daughters.
  • Jack-o'-lantern: Lilia's safe American childhood. Cut when the war news breaks, smashed on Halloween.

Irony

The opposite of what you'd expect.
  • School teaches the American Revolution while a real war for independence is on their TV every night (Mrs. Kenyon takes the Pakistan book away).
  • He's 'not Indian', yet he speaks, eats and looks just like Lilia's family.
  • The happy ending (his family is safe) is a loss for Lilia: no more dinners, she misses him.

Foreshadowing

A hint of something that happens later.
  • The knife slips and ruins the pumpkin's face right when the news says India may join the war: violence is coming.
  • Mr. Pirzada's fear on Halloween and the smashed pumpkin hint at the war breaking out weeks later (Dec 4).

Imagery

Words that make you see, hear or feel the scene.
  • The war on the TV inside a cozy living room; children Lilia's age in Dacca.
  • The watch beside his dinner plate; the smashed pumpkin in the street.
  • Use it as: "The image of ___ makes the scene feel ___."

Motif

An image or idea that keeps coming back.
  • Dinner and food (a motif because it shows up again and again, not just once): the title says he "came to dine", he eats with the family every night, he brings candy every night, and his thank-you card at the end is about their meals.
  • What it means (this is the part that makes it a motif and not just a detail): sharing food turns a stranger into family. The dinner table is where an Indian family and a Pakistani man ignore the border and treat each other as the same.
  • How it changes (a motif can grow): early on, dinner is a normal routine. During the war it is also where he watches the news with them. At the end, when his family is safe, the dinners stop, so the absence of food shows the loss.
  • Say it like this: "The motif of dinner and food shows that sharing meals builds community and belonging. It keeps coming back (the nightly dinners, the candy, the card), and it proves that Mr. Pirzada is treated like family even though the world says he is a foreigner."
  • Time (a motif because clocks and days keep being counted): the watch on Dacca time, the 11 hours ahead, the 12 days of war, "miles and hours away". It shows he is stuck waiting for news of his family and that his mind is far from the table.
  • Say it like this: "The motif of time, like his watch set to Dacca, shows he lives in two places at once and can only wait."

Contrast / juxtaposition

Two things put side by side to show the difference.
  • Safe home in Boston vs war in Dacca.
  • Kids in witch costumes for fun vs Mr. Pirzada's real fear for his daughters.
  • Sweet candy vs violent news.

Point of view

Who tells the story and how.
  • First person, Lilia. She's 10 in the story but tells it as an adult looking back (retrospective narrator).
  • So we see the war through a child's confused eyes, with the grown-up's understanding at the end.

Characterization

How the author shows what a character is like.
  • Indirect: Mr. Pirzada's actions show him: polite, neat, brings candy every night, sets the watch, worries about Lilia on Halloween. Caring and anxious.
  • Lilia changes: confused at the start, praying every night, missing him at the end (dynamic character).

Setting

When and where, and why it matters.
  • Autumn 1971, near Boston, during the war that creates Bangladesh. The faraway setting (Dacca) is felt only through TV, the watch and his worry.
